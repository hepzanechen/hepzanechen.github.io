A melody moves forward. Before the next note arrives, we seem to be waiting for it. When it finally lands exactly where we imagined, we feel satisfied—as though a sentence has been completed or something suspended has settled.

This experience contains two questions: **Why do we predict what comes next in music? And why can a fulfilled prediction feel good?**

They need separate answers. Being able to predict a sound does not mean liking it. Hearing something unexpected does not necessarily mean disliking it. Musical enjoyment often lies between knowing what will happen and wanting it to happen.

## You have learned music, even without studying theory

Hearing a song for the first time does not mean encountering its musical language for the first time.

Nursery rhymes, pop songs, film scores, and rhythmic patterns provide extensive experience with what occurs together, what follows what, and what makes an ending sound final. You may recognize what a chord does without knowing its name.

This gradual acquisition of regularities, without explicitly memorizing rules, can be studied as **implicit statistical learning**. “Statistical” does not mean that the brain literally draws up frequency tables. It means that recurring distributions and relationships in the input influence subsequent judgments.

Musical expectations have at least three sources:

- **Memory for this piece**: knowing the next line of a familiar chorus.
- **Long-term listening experience**: never having heard this song, but having heard many in related styles.
- **Regularities in the current passage**: hearing a rhythm or motif repeat a few times and beginning to anticipate another occurrence.

Sensory relationships between sounds also contribute. Long-term structural knowledge and short-term acoustic information can be modeled and compared separately; neither should be reduced to remembering a particular song.[1]

A 2024 study constructed novel melodies in a scale outside established musical cultures to investigate how preferences emerge. Across nine studies involving 1,185 adults, exposure frequency and structural prediction errors affected liking, with similar learning trajectories in U.S. and Chinese participants. This supports the formation of musical preferences through learning. It does not establish that all expectations are learned or that all cultures share the same preferences.[2]

Part of what makes a continuation seem inevitable may therefore be that **we have already learned its musical language without noticing**.

## A prediction need not specify an exact note

Prediction does not always amount to an internal musical score.

We may expect a phrase to descend, a beat to return at a certain moment, or a musical sentence to continue rather than stop. Pitch, rhythm, harmony, and phrase structure can support expectations at different timescales.

A better description is a set of tendencies toward possible continuations. Sometimes one outcome dominates. Sometimes several seem plausible.

This distinguishes **uncertainty before an event** from **surprise after the event occurs**. They are related, but different. You can be unsure what comes next and hear a reasonably familiar continuation; or you can feel certain and encounter an unexpected turn.

Another distinction matters:

> “I think it will continue this way” concerns probability. “I want it to continue this way” already involves preference.

We can accurately predict an irritating ringtone without enjoying it. An account of prediction therefore still needs an account of value.

## Why does “finally” feel satisfying?

Start with a reasonably well-supported finding: music can engage reward-related mechanisms.

A 2011 PET and fMRI study found striatal dopamine release during intensely pleasurable musical experiences, with different regional involvement during anticipation and peak emotion. Enjoyment is therefore not confined to the moment a climax arrives. But findings about intense responses should not automatically be generalized to every mildly pleasing continuation.[3]

In 2019, a double-blind, within-subject pharmacological study tested 27 participants under levodopa, risperidone, and placebo in separate sessions. It reported bidirectional changes in musical pleasure and motivation, providing causal evidence for dopamine's involvement in musical reward.[4]

Yet **dopamine's involvement does not explain why this particular relationship between sounds becomes rewarding**. Nor does it imply that every correctly predicted note triggers a dose of happiness.

One candidate explanation concerns the unfolding process: a passage establishes tension, delays a continuation, and eventually reaches a resolution. An ending acquires significance from what preceded it.

The same note may seem unremarkable in isolation but deeply satisfying after a particular preparation. Studying only its acoustic properties could miss an important part of the experience.

“Resolving tension,” “making predictions more reliable,” and “processing more fluently” are possible explanations. They are not synonyms, and an evocative example does not establish any one of them as the mechanism.

## If correct predictions feel good, why isn't more repetition always better?

Any explanation must face this question.

In 2019, Cheung and colleagues studied expectations for Western pop harmony, using a statistical model to estimate uncertainty and surprise. In their task, relatively high pleasure could arise when an expected chord followed an uncertain context, but also when a surprising chord followed a context that supported confident expectations.[5]

These findings concern **Western tonal harmony, particular stimuli, and a particular rating task**. They are not universal rules for every listener and melody.

A 2025 melody study found an inverted-U relationship between predictability and preference in two independent samples. Neither extreme predictability nor extreme unpredictability was necessarily preferred. The preferred degree of surprise also varied with musical uncertainty and individual differences.[6]

The specific relationships in these studies are not identical and should not be assembled into one universal formula. Their shared implication is that **pleasure cannot be determined solely by whether a prediction was correct**. Context, prior certainty, surprise, learning history, and the listener all matter.

## Three different senses of “good”

Discussions often conflate three judgments:

- **Acoustic consonance**: how simultaneous sounds interact at a sensory level.
- **Structural fit**: whether a note sounds natural, prepared, or resolving within the current phrase.
- **Subjective liking**: whether this listener enjoys the experience and wants to keep listening.

A consonant chord can sound misplaced. A dissonant sound can be compelling in context. The relationship between consonance and musical emotion requires attention to both sensory and cognitive mechanisms.[7]

When we describe something as harmonious, we should ask: Does it sound smooth? Does it complete a structure? Or do we particularly like the turn it takes?

## Why can a song remain moving after a hundred listens?

This also challenges the claim that musical pleasure comes entirely from surprise.

With familiar music, we may know precisely when a climax will arrive and still anticipate it eagerly. Predicting the sound and looking forward to experiencing it again are compatible.

Separating the contributions of structural resolution, memory, bodily responses, and personal associations requires further experimental design. Continued enjoyment under repetition is not exclusive evidence for any one theory.

Likewise, “prediction helps survival, so evolution rewards us for predicting melodies” offers a possible ultimate explanation. It is not a conclusion established by the experiments discussed here. Explaining a present-day mechanism differs from explaining its evolutionary origins.

## What could we compare by listening?

Give the same short musical introduction three endings: an immediate resolution, a delayed resolution, and a turn in another direction. Ask separately:

1. Did this match my expectation?
2. Did it sound finished?
3. Did I like it?

This is a demonstration for distinguishing experiences, not a formal experiment. Testing explanations would also require controlling loudness, timbre, rhythm, familiarity, and consonance; balancing presentation order; and measuring probability judgments separately from liking. In particular, we should not first select attractive endings and then explain their attractiveness by labeling them expected.

Starting from that moment of “finally,” we find several threads rather than a single switch: how sounds are predicted, how structures acquire meaning, and how experiences acquire value.

**We hear not only the music already sounding, but also the music we are waiting for. Fulfilled predictions can be satisfying, and part of music's appeal lies in how it teaches us to wait again.**
